Two words that sound alike. Authentication answers who are you, and happens once, at sign-in. Authorization answers what may you do, and is checked for every action. A hotel helps: the front desk checks your ID once; your key card opens only your room, every time you use it. Budi is a confirmed user, but his key does not open the users list.

After a correct sign-in the back end creates a session: a pass it remembers, linked to Budi. The browser shows that pass with every later request, so Budi does not type his password again. Without a valid pass the back end answers 401, meaning not signed in. Signing out ends the pass. Keep it at this level: the pass is a session kept by the back end; do not teach token formats.

Two roles keep the practice small. Both can do everything they do today, including the shared watchlist. Only an Admin can see the list of users, promote or demote someone, and see usage. Point out where roles live: in the app's own database, not in the directory. The directory says who you are; the app decides what you may do. That is why the admin page can change roles.

The audit trail answers the question from the start of the session: who changed the watchlist? Every important action becomes one line with time, user, action, target and result. Lines are only added; the app offers no way to edit or delete them. The admin page reads the same records to show usage: the number of users, sign-ins today and the latest activity.

Record actions, not secrets. The five event types on the left are enough for the practice. Never record passwords, session passes, cookies or request bodies. Remind participants that audit records can themselves hold personal data, such as names and activity times, so keep them minimal and show them only to Admins. In a real bank system, the risk and IT teams set retention and access rules for logs.
